Introduction

In this third part of my tutorial series on statistical interactions, we are going to push further on our understanding and reporting of interactions. Since we are going beyond the realm of linear models by exploring generalized linear models (GLMs) and their variants, I am assuming here that the reader has a basic understanding of how GLMs work especially concerning their use of link functions.

I typically write up these tutorials based on bits and pieces of information I have gathered here and there but I will (in part) address topics covered specifically by Spake et al. (2023) https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/brv.12939 and Mize (2019) https://sociologicalscience.com/articles-v6-4-81/ while giving examples of tools that can be used to report interaction effects on the additive and multiplicative scale.

The case studies in this tutorial will focus on species occurence and abundance. We will first look at habitat suitability for a given species to occur in a given location. Second, we will explore factors that affect their abundance when they are present. Third, we will incorporate both these processes in a single model and learn how to extract as much information as possible from these types of analyses.

Case Study 1: Species occurence

In this first case study, we are interested in species occurence in different habitats. Specifically, we want evaluate the the interacting effects of habitat composition and environmental variables on the probability of occurence in a given patch. For this tutorial, I am using functions from the distributional package to simulate the distribution of predictors. Rather than drawing straight from base-R functions like rnorm()/rgamma(), we first describe a distribution as an object, then generate values from it:

dist_normal(mu, sd), dist_gamma(shape, rate), dist_beta(shape1, shape2), dist_poisson(lambda) — each builds a distribution object with those parameters, without drawing any numbers yet

dist_mixture(dist_1, dist_2, weights = c(w1, w2)) — combines two or more distribution objects into a mixture (e.g., a bimodal “tree cover” variable built from two beta distributions, one skewed low and one skewed high)

generate(dist_object, n) — draws n random values from a distribution object (the equivalent of calling rnorm()/rgamma()/etc. directly, but works uniformly across any distribution you built with the functions above)

Why bother with this instead of just calling rbeta()/rgamma() directly? Mainly convenience when a predictor’s distribution is more complex than a single named family — dist_mixture() in particular makes it easy to build a bimodal or multimodal predictor (like patches clustering at low vs. high tree cover) by combining two standard distributions, something that’s awkward to do with base-R r*() functions alone. As with the rest of the simulation code, you don’t need to be able to construct one of these from scratch — just recognize dist_*() as “defining a distribution” and generate() as “drawing numbers from it.”

A tool I really appreciate to understand different distributions and what they look like with different parameter values is the Shiny app The dsitribution zoo that can be found here https://ben18785.shinyapps.io/distribution-zoo/.

Notice something strange: we simulated the effect of tree cover on occurrence to be 0.45, but summary(mod_1) reports a slope of about 1.87 for tree_cov. Did the model get it wrong? No — it’s reporting the effect on a different scale.

When we fit a binomial GLM with a logit link, the model isn’t drawing a straight line through probabilities. Instead, it draws a straight line through the log-odds of occurrence, and then bends that line into an S-shape (between 0 and 1) to get back to a probability. The number 1.87 is the slope on that log-odds scale. It’s a real, meaningful number, but it’s not directly comparable to our simulated 0.45, which was the slope on the probability scale.

Here’s the key idea: because the logit link bends the line, how much a one-unit increase in tree cover changes the probability of occurrence depends on where you are on the curve. Near the middle of the S-shape (probability around 50%), a small push on the log-odds scale translates into a fairly large push on the probability scale. Near the top or bottom of the S-shape (probability near 0% or 100%), that same push on the log-odds scale barely moves the probability at all, because the curve has flattened out.

We can reconstruct that logic by hand from summary(mod_1)’s coefficients, without calling slopes() at all. The trick is the derivative of the inverse-logit function: if \(\eta\) is the log-odds (the linear predictor) and \(p = \text{inv\_logit}(\eta)\), then

\[\frac{dp}{d\,\text{tree\_cov}} = p(1-p) \times \beta_{\text{tree\_cov}}\]

where \(\beta_{\text{tree\_cov}}\) is the 1.87 log-odds coefficient from summary(mod_1). In words: take the raw log-odds slope and shrink it by \(p(1-p)\), a quantity that is largest (0.25) when \(p = 0.5\) and shrinks toward 0 as \(p\) approaches 0 or 1 — exactly the “flattening near the ends of the S-curve” idea from above.

# Pull the log-odds coefficient straight from summary(mod_1)
beta_tree <- fixef(mod_1)$cond["tree_cov"]

# Log-odds prediction (eta) at the same covariate values used above
eta <- predict(
  mod_1,
  newdata = datagrid(model = mod_1, temp = mean(occ_data$temp), rain = mean(occ_data$rain)),
  type = "link"
)

# Convert eta to a probability, then apply the p(1-p) correction
p <- plogis(eta)
p * (1 - p) * beta_tree

0.4666768

This hand-computed value should line up closely with the slopes() output above — both are asking “how much does predicted probability move for a small change in tree cover, evaluated at these particular values of temp and rain?” The only difference is that slopes() does this calculation (and its standard errors) for us automatically.

This is exactly why slopes(mod_1, variables = "tree", newdata = datagrid(temp = mean(occ_data$temp), rain = mean(occ_data$rain))) gives us back something close to 0.45. Rather than reporting the abstract log-odds slope, slopes() asks: “at these specific values of temp and rain, how much does the predicted probability change when tree cover increases a little?” That’s a probability-scale answer, so it’s directly comparable to the number we used to simulate the data.

There’s a subtle but important consequence here: because the amount of “bending” depends on where you are on the S-curve, the probability-scale effect of tree cover also depends on the values of rain and temp — even though we never told the model to include an interaction between them! This is what we mean by an implicit interaction. The logit link function automatically makes every predictor’s effect (on the probability scale) depend on the values of all the other predictors, simply because of the shape of the S-curve. We’ll see this play out visually below, where the estimated slope for tree cover changes depending on the combination of rain and temp we plug in.

Spake et al. (2023) make a related point in the ecology literature: when we ask “does an interaction exist?”, the answer can depend on which measurement scale we ask the question on — additive (e.g., probability, or log-odds treated as a straight difference) versus multiplicative (e.g., ratios, or a logarithmic scale). Getting the scale question wrong is one of the ways they describe interactions being mis-detected or mis-sized (what they call a “Type-D” error). Our tree-cover example is a small, concrete illustration of exactly this idea: there is no interaction term of any kind in mod_1’s formula, yet whether or not you can see an interaction between tree cover and the other predictors depends entirely on whether you’re looking at the log-odds scale (no interaction, by construction) or the probability scale (an interaction appears, because of the S-curve).

Two things are worth unpacking here, both stemming from the fact that we now have three predictors instead of two.

First, unlike the numerical × numerical tutorial’s two-predictor case, we can’t show “the slope of tree_cov across all values of the other predictors” in a single 2D plot anymore — there isn’t one “other predictor” left over once we pick tree_cov as the focal variable, there are two (rain and temp). The approach above handles this by producing one plot per moderator, holding the remaining predictor(s) fixed at their mean each time (e.g., the temp panels hold rain at its mean; the rain panels hold temp at its mean). That’s a reasonable default, but it does mean each plot only shows one cross-section of a genuinely three-dimensional (or higher) surface. A few alternatives worth considering as the number of predictors grows:

  • Facet by a third variable (as we did earlier with facet_grid(rain ~ temp)) instead of holding it fixed at the mean, so you can see whether the shape of the moderating trend itself changes across the third predictor’s range — at the cost of more panels to scan.
  • Small multiples at representative values of the “other” predictors (e.g., their five-number summary, via datagrid(temp = fivenum)) rather than a single mean, to check whether conclusions drawn “at the mean” generalize to more extreme, but still plausible, conditions.
  • A single average marginal trend (avg_slopes(..., by = "temp") without fixing rain) that integrates over the observed distribution of all remaining predictors instead of fixing them — trading a harder-to-interpret “held constant at” statement for one number per value of the focal moderator that better reflects your actual sample.

Second, and more central to the point of this section: notice that on the link (log-odds) scale, the slope of tree_cov is a flat, constant ~1.87 line across the full range of both temp and rain — exactly as it should be, since mod_1’s formula has no interaction term at all. On the response (probability) scale, by contrast, the slope visibly curves with temperature (and to a lesser extent with rain, since its true simulated effect is much weaker). This is the implicit interaction made concrete: the moderators only “act” on the probability scale, purely through the curvature of the logit link, never on the log-odds scale the model was actually fit on.

Since temperature and rain implicitely modulate the effect of tree cover on species occurence, we might be interested in the “overall” effect of tree cover across temperature and rain conditions. We can define this effect as the average effect of tree cover. marginaleffects is helpful in doing that with its avg_predictions and avg_slopes functions.

Already, we are seeing a myriad of ways such a simple model can yield information. What are the effects we want to report and how should we report them? Do we want the conditional effect of each variable (the effect of a focal variable when the others are set to their mean) or the marginal (average) effect or the degree to which a focal variable’s effect varies at different values of the other predictors (implicit interaction)? Hopefully by now, the reader has started to notice that the model summary that is typically reported answers none of these objectives. I follow many people’s philosophy of interpreting visual representations of the model predictions as these are the ones most intuitive and useful. However, I am aware that most fields appreciate results presented in the form of a table. Luckily, there is no obligation in reporting the output obtained from the model summary via summary(). In this tutorial, I will try to suggest different results tables. For this example, let’s report the average effect of our three predictors on the probability scale, their 95% confidence intervals, p-values (…) and model R^2 value. I still find these slope values difficult to interpret as we know probability does not increase linearly until infinity (even though our simulation code assumes so; we will fix this later on). If we want the slope on the log-odds scale, we can change type = "response" to type = "link" in the avg_slopes() function below.

Tjur’s R² here is about 0.16. Unlike the pseudo-R² measures built for continuous outcomes, Tjur’s R² is designed specifically for binary models: it’s simply the difference between the average predicted probability among the observed “occurrence” cases and the average predicted probability among the observed “absence” cases. A value of 0 would mean the model’s predictions don’t separate the two groups at all, and 1 would mean perfect separation. A value around 0.16 indicates fairly modest discrimination — mod_1’s predicted probabilities for plots where the species is present are, on average, only somewhat higher than for plots where it’s absent. That’s broadly consistent with what we simulated: tree cover has a detectable and fairly precisely estimated effect (its CI excludes zero), but rain and temperature contribute comparatively little, and substantial randomness (the Bernoulli draw) still governs whether the species is observed at any given plot.

The same story repeats itself. summary(mod_2) reports a tree_cov coefficient that is not 4.5 — because a Poisson GLM with a log link draws a straight line through the log of the expected count, not through the expected count itself. To turn a log-scale prediction back into an actual expected count, we exponentiate it, and that exponentiation is what bends a straight line into a curve that always stays positive (counts can never be negative).

Because of that curve, a fixed log-scale coefficient translates into a different count-scale slope depending on where you evaluate it — exactly the same phenomenon we saw with the logistic curve. This time, the local rate of change works out to \[\lambda \cdot \beta\] (the expected count itself, times the log-scale coefficient), instead of \[p(1-p) \cdot \beta\] When we plug in the average rain and temp values via slopes(), we get back a count-scale slope for tree_cov close to our simulated 4.5.

This hand-computed value should land close to both the slopes() output above and our simulated 4.5 — exponentiating the linear predictor gives us \[\lambda\] and multiplying by the raw log-scale coefficient \[\beta\] converts it into the local count-scale slope.

And once again, this produces an implicit interaction: the count-scale effect of tree cover depends on the values of rain and temp (through their influence on the expected count) \[\lambda\] even though the model formula never specified an interaction term. Any log-link GLM will behave this way — additive on the log scale automatically becomes multiplicative (and interaction-like) on the response scale.

This is the same additive-versus-multiplicative distinction that Spake et al. (2023) highlight as a routinely overlooked property of statistical interactions: whether a response is being analyzed on an additive scale or a multiplicative one (such as a ratio or logarithmic scale). A log-link Poisson model is additive on the log scale by construction — that’s the whole point of the link function — but that same additivity becomes multiplicative on the count scale, since exponentiating a sum turns it into a product \[e^{a+b} = e^a \times e^b\] Two predictors that don’t interact at all on the log scale will therefore appear to interact on the count scale, for the same reason the abstract flags as a source of “Type-D” errors: judging whether or how strongly an interaction exists depends on which scale you’re looking at.

This is the visual counterpart of the \[\lambda \cdot \beta\] formula above: the steepness of the curve at any point is \[\lambda\] (the expected count at that point), so multiplying by \[\beta\] converts the log-scale slope into the local count-scale slope shown here.

Just like the logit model, the log-link Poisson model gives us two complementary ways to summarize a predictor’s effect. The local, additive slope \[\lambda \cdot \beta\] in units of “individuals per unit of the predictor” is useful when you want to say how many more or fewer individuals to expect at a specific point on the curve — but because the curve bends, that number is only valid near the covariate values it was evaluated at, and it changes as those values change (the implicit interaction we saw above). The rate ratio \[e^\beta\] by contrast, is a multiplicative, percent-change interpretation that holds everywhere on the curve, regardless of the values of the other predictors: a one-unit increase in a predictor always multiplies expected abundance by the same factor \[e^\beta\] no matter what rain and temp are set to. This stability is exactly because the rate ratio lives on the same (log) scale the model is additive on — exponentiating a constant additive effect always yields a constant multiplicative one. That makes the rate ratio a natural choice when we want one summary number per predictor that doesn’t depend on where the other predictors are set, at the cost of it being harder to translate into a concrete “how many individuals” statement.

Case Study 3: occurence and abundance all at once

In the two previous case studies, we looked at the occurence of a species in 200 sampled plots and the abundance of that species when it is present in 200 plots. This does not entirely reflect what we would obtain in reality. If we were to sample 200 plots, a species would have a given probability of being present in a plot, depending on a baseline occurence probability and the effect of our predictors. Then, within the subset of plots in which the species occurs (most likely less than 200), we would expect a given (non-zero) number of individuals to be present depending on the effect of some predictors. If a predictor affects both species occurence and abundance, we might be interested in how it affects abundance across all plots (its combined effect on occurence and abundance). Our research question here is three-fold: What affects the occurence of species in our sampled plots?; when present, what affects its abundance?; what affects both occurence and abundance simultaneously?

We can address these questions with a model class called Hurdle Models. A hurdle model combines two sub-models: a binomial (logistic) component that models whether a value clears the “hurdle” of zero (occurence) and a truncated count component (e.g., a zero-truncated Poisson) that models the value given that it is non-zero (abundance when present). The two components are fit jointly but are conceptually separate processes, each with its own predictors, coefficients, and link function.

This gives us two complementary ways to interpret the model. We can look at each part separately: the occurence (binomial) part tells us what drives presence/absence, and the abundance (count) part tells us what drives the number of individuals given that the species is present. Or, we can combine both parts to get predictions on the population scale: the expected abundance across all plots, whether or not the species is present there, obtained by multiplying the predicted probability of occurence by the predicted abundance when present. This combined quantity is what marginaleffects returns by default (type = "response") and is usually what we care about when asking how a predictor affects abundance across the whole landscape.

Up until now, we have been considering implicit interactions (on the response scale) between predictors but in hurdle models, there are implicit interactions between the model parts. The effect of tree cover on the abundance of the population of plots depends on its effect on occurence and abundance when present.

Let’s push further into interactions by simulating a new example. In this case, we have a species that will select a habitat patch based on the percent tree cover in that patch. The more covered the patch, the more likely it is to be selected. Once a patch is selected, tree cover does not have an effect on population growth rate. Prey availability (number of prey individuals) will make the population increase (on average). When there are individuals of another species competing for these prey, the per-capita feeding rate will be reduced and so will population growth rate. Also, the competitors are territorial and defend territories that overlap with our focal species of interest they having a direct impact on population growth. We are interested here in population dynamics (rate of increase) and, from a conservation stand point, we want to know where to target our efforts: should we reduce competition, increase prey abundance and/or increase tree cover to increase raw population size? In what conditions should we act?

Before jumping into the simulation code, it’s worth pausing on a detail that changes here compared to every earlier simulation in this tutorial. In Case Studies 1 and 2, and in the simple hurdle example just above, we generated prob and lambda by plugging a linear combination of predictors directly into rbinom() and rpois() — e.g. prob = occurence_intercept + occurence_tree_effect * tree_cov. We flagged at the time that this was a simplification: a real probability or rate is never truly an untransformed linear function of predictors, because nothing stops a linear combination from wandering outside the range that probability (0 to 1) or a Poisson rate (must be positive) can actually take. With small coefficients and predictors confined to a narrow range, that simplification stayed harmless — prob and lambda happened to stay inside their valid bounds — but it was always a shortcut, not the mechanistically correct way to simulate this kind of data.

From here on, we simulate the right way: by applying the model’s own inverse link function to the linear predictor, exactly as a fitted GLM would if we ran the process in reverse. For the Poisson (log-link) component, that means wrapping the linear predictor in exp(), so lambda = exp(1 + 0.12 * prey - ...) is guaranteed to be positive no matter what values prey and competitors take. For the binomial (logit-link) component, that means wrapping the linear predictor in plogis() (the inverse logit), so prob = plogis(-2 + 0.45 * tree_cov) is guaranteed to stay between 0 and 1. This is the same \[p(1-p)\cdot\beta\] and \[\lambda \cdot \beta\] machinery from Case Studies 1 and 2, just applied in the forward direction — simulating data the way the link function actually generates it, rather than approximating it with an untransformed linear predictor that only happens to behave well in a narrow range.

Since tree cover is a percentage (bound between zero and 1) whereas the other two predictors are counts between 0 and approximately 13. The fact that the predictors are on different scales hinders our interpretation of model coefficients. Now we fit the hurdle model. Recall the story we simulated: tree cover governs whether a plot is occupied (the zero-inflation/hurdle part), while prey and competitors govern how many individuals are present once a plot is occupied (the conditional, truncated-Poisson part) — and crucially, we simulated an explicit prey * competitors interaction on the log scale (-0.045 * prey * competitors), on top of the implicit interactions the log and logit links introduce on their own. We fit that explicit interaction directly in the conditional formula, using the scaled predictors so the coefficients are comparable to one another and the intercept reflects abundance at average prey/competitor levels:

The scl_prey:scl_competitors coefficient (about -0.18) is on the log scale — the scale the conditional model is additive on. Just like beta_tree and beta_tree_2 earlier in the tutorial, this number isn’t directly interpretable as “individuals gained or lost,” and now there’s an added wrinkle: it’s an interaction coefficient, so even its sign doesn’t translate simply onto the count scale on its own. Here scl_prey’s main effect (about -0.10) isn’t distinguishable from zero on its own — a reminder that in the presence of an interaction, a main-effect coefficient describes the slope only where the interacting variable (scl_competitors) sits at zero, not “the effect of prey” in general. scl_competitors’s main effect is clearly negative (about -0.76: more competitors, fewer individuals), and the negative interaction compounds that further: whatever positive effect prey has is strongest when competitors are scarce, and fades (or reverses) as competitor density rises. Before trying to reason about this from the coefficients alone, let’s look at the response (count) scale and the rate-ratio (link) scale directly, exactly as we did for mod_2.

The interaction on the count scale

The interaction on the rate-ratio scale

The count-scale picture above mixes together the explicit prey * competitors interaction and the implicit curvature from exponentiating the linear predictor (the same \[\lambda \cdot \beta\] logic from Case Study 2). To isolate something closer to “the interaction itself,” we move to the rate-ratio scale: for each one-SD increase in competitors, by what factor does the effect of one extra SD of prey get multiplied? That’s exp() of the interaction coefficient, and because it lives on the model’s own additive (log) scale, it doesn’t depend on where competitors are evaluated — unlike the count-scale slope above.

A symmetric interaction, viewed from the other predictor

Spake et al. (2023) make a point that’s easy to lose sight of once a plot like the one above has become the “default” way of showing an interaction: a two-way interaction term is, mathematically, perfectly symmetric. scl_prey:scl_competitors is the exact same coefficient as scl_competitors:scl_prey — there is no sense in which the model treats prey as “the” focal variable and competitors as “the” moderator, or vice versa. That choice is entirely ours, made for narrative convenience (we picked prey as the x-axis because the conservation question was framed around “how much does prey availability help”), not because the statistics privilege one direction over the other. Spake et al. flag this as a common source of asymmetric or incomplete interaction reporting: papers will show (and discuss at length) the effect of A moderated by B, but never show the mathematically identical effect of B moderated by A, even though the two views can highlight different, equally valid practical takeaways.

Here, showing the interaction the other way around — competitors on the x-axis, with one line per level of prey’s five-number summary — makes a point that’s much harder to see in the prey-on-the-x-axis version: regardless of how much prey is available, a sufficiently high density of competitors drives expected abundance down to roughly the same low floor. The lines, which fan out dramatically at low competitor density (echoing the steep low-competitor slopes we found earlier), converge almost on top of each other once competitors exceed about 4–5 individuals.

Average predictions: collapsing the interaction into one curve per predictor

Everything up to this point has described the interaction conditionally — the effect of prey at a specific level of competitors, or vice versa. Sometimes we instead want a single summary curve per predictor that integrates over the other predictor’s (and tree cover’s) observed distribution, the same “average effect” idea from avg_predictions()/avg_slopes() that we used for mod_1 back in Case Study 1 — there, a one-line call like avg_predictions(mod_1, variables = "tree_cov") was all we needed. Here, we instead build the grid explicitly with datagrid(..., grid_type = "counterfactual") before handing it to avg_predictions(..., by = "scl_prey"). Why the extra step, and is it actually doing something different?

It’s worth checking directly, because the two approaches can look deceptively similar. avg_predictions(variables = "focal") is really shorthand: internally, it builds a grid that crosses the requested sequence of the focal variable against every observed row of the data — exactly a counterfactual grid — and then averages the resulting predictions within each focal value. That’s a different (and, for our purposes, more correct) procedure than simply calling datagrid(focal = seq(...)) by itself, which instead holds every other predictor fixed at a single representative value (the mean, for numeric predictors) and gives you the prediction at that one point, not an average over the sample. This is A LOT of figures and information. And we have not even plotted these effects on the rate-ratio scale or log-odds scale. What should we report in our publication? This all depends on the research question. If we want to make recommendations about how we can better conserve our species by acting on either of the three variables we focused on, we could report the figures we have generated above which relate more to raw abundance increase. To answer questions relating to how these variables affect population dynamics (on a rate scale), we could report these in the form of a table as these rates are assumed to be linear in these models and are thus easier to interpret in a table form compared to potentially non-linear relationships on the probability and count scales.

A full rate-ratio results table, by model component

Let’s build that table, following the same approach as mod2_tbl in Case Study 2: pull every fixed-effect slope on the link scale and exponentiate it into a rate ratio, keeping coefficients standardized throughout so they’re directly comparable to one another. mod_hu has two components — the conditional (truncated-Poisson) part describing abundance when present, and the zero-inflation (binomial) part describing occupancy — so we report rate ratios separately for each, with a clear label for which component (and, where relevant, which moderator value) each row describes.

For scl_tree_cov in the zero-inflation part, a quick note on interpretation: glmmTMB’s zero-inflation formula models the probability of a structural zero (the plot being unsuitable/unoccupied), not the probability of occurrence directly. So exp() of that coefficient is an odds ratio for becoming a structural zero, not a rate ratio for abundance — a rate ratio below 1 here means higher tree cover is associated with lower odds of the plot being a structural zero, i.e. higher occupancy, consistent with how we simulated the data.

Because scl_prey and scl_competitors interact, neither has a single rate ratio that applies everywhere — exactly the implicit-interaction-on-top-of-explicit-interaction idea from earlier in this case study. So, following the same scheme we used for temp’s effect at low vs. high tree cover back in Case Study 2, we report each one’s rate ratio at three representative levels of the other (-1 SD, 0 SD, +1 SD), plus the interaction term itself — the ratio of these rate ratios per 1-SD increase in the moderator — as a single row that doesn’t depend on where the other predictor is evaluated.

What we learned

  1. Link functions create implicit interactions: Even without an explicit interaction term, a non-linear link function (logit, log, …) makes every predictor’s effect on the response scale depend on the values of all the other predictors. This is purely a consequence of the curvature of the link, not evidence of a “real” interaction in the data-generating process.

  2. The scale you report on changes what you see: On the link scale, effects are additive and (without an explicit interaction term) perfectly flat across the other predictors. On the response scale, the same model can show a strongly curving, moderator-dependent effect. Whether an interaction “exists” can depend entirely on which scale you ask the question on (Spake et al.’s additive vs. multiplicative distinction).

  3. Local slopes vs. scale-invariant ratios: A response-scale slope (e.g., individuals per unit of a predictor, or probability points per unit) only applies at the specific covariate values it was evaluated at. A rate ratio \[e^\beta\] odds ratio, etc. on the link scale is constant everywhere on the curve, which makes it a natural summary number when you want one value per predictor that doesn’t depend on where the other predictors are set — at the cost of being harder to translate into a concrete “how many individuals” statement.

  4. Interactions are symmetric: A two-way interaction term describes a single, mathematically symmetric relationship between two predictors. Showing only “A moderated by B” and never “B moderated by A” risks reporting just half of the story, even though both views are equally valid and can emphasize different practical conclusions.

  5. Hurdle and zero-inflated models split a response into separate processes: A hurdle model lets us interpret the occurrence (binomial) and abundance-when-present (truncated count) parts separately, each with its own predictors and link function, or combine them into a single population-scale prediction (occurrence probability × conditional abundance). Both views are useful, depending on whether the research question is about presence, abundance given presence, or abundance across the whole landscape.

Tools for interpretation

  • slopes() / avg_slopes() with type = "response" vs. type = "link": Compare a predictor’s effect on the natural (probability, count) scale against its effect on the scale the model is additive on.
  • predictions() / avg_predictions() with type = "conditional", "response", or "zprob": For hurdle/zero-inflated models, get predictions from just the count part, the full population-scale combination, or the probability of a structural zero.
  • datagrid(..., grid_type = "counterfactual"): Average predictions or slopes over the observed distribution of the other predictors, rather than holding them at a single representative value (e.g., their mean).
  • Exponentiating link-scale slopes (exp(estimate)): Turn a log-odds or log-count coefficient into an odds ratio or rate ratio that stays constant regardless of where the other predictors are evaluated.
  • fivenum() and density peaks (density()): Choose informative, data-driven values of a moderator (e.g., for faceting or line colors) instead of arbitrary round numbers, especially for bimodal predictors like tree cover in this tutorial.
